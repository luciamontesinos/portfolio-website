My PhD is titled Designing for Participatory Music-making and explores, through a set of three designed artefacts, how to get people to engage in music-making. I did it at the IT University of Copenhagen. My defense is still pending. 

## About the project
Many people believe music-making is reserved for the talented few. My PhD starts from the opposite view: music is something people do, from performing in a concert hall to humming to yourself, so taking part shouldn't depend on skill. I explored how to design for that through three projects. Each combined something I built with a study of how people actually played with it.

- **Tubularium**: a human-scale instrument of glowing tubes for making melodies with your whole body.
- **Picnic**: a picnic basket of everyday objects that you strike to build rhythms, with a machine-learning model that is wrong on purpose. Best Paper, ACM DIS 2026.
- **Table Manners**: giant dinnerware that people strike while voicing the sounds themselves.

Across the three projects, two lessons stood out for designing experiences that anyone can join:
**Invite, then support.** People first need a reason to start: something curious, familiar-but-strange, or simply fun. Once they've started, they need support that makes them feel capable. That support can be technical (scales and loops that make everything sound good), but it can also be social and experiential, such as an absurd setting that lowers the stakes.
**Leave room for the unknown.** You can't anticipate every body, skill or expectation. Good designs set the conditions for interaction without dictating how it unfolds, and leave space for people to find their own way in.


## Abstract

Many people believe music-making should be reserved for the talented few. This may stem from the way much of Western thought has come to treat music as an abstract object, giving primacy to the piece as an ideal that any performance can only approximate. In opposition, the concept of musicking understands music as an activity, giving primacy to performance in its broadest sense – from a concert hall to banging on pots and pans or humming to yourself. Taking part in music-making is then defined not by one's ability to approximate such an ideal, but by engaging in the activity itself. 

Building on this view, this thesis understands music-making as a situated and embodied activity, drawing attention to the relationships between people, materials, technologies, and the contexts in which music-making takes place. Bridging perspectives from Human-Computer Interaction and Music Interaction, this thesis explores how to design for these relationships so that anyone can take part in music-making. I refer to this as participatory music-making and follow a Research-through-Design approach to develop this research programme through three design experiments, each combining an artefact with a study of how participants engaged with it.

The first artefact, Tubularium, is an interactive music system designed to facilitate melodic music-making through embodied interaction, built on the premise that it should require no initial skill while still eliciting agency and providing a meaningful experience. The second artefact, Picnic, is an interactive music installation that invites participants to build rhythms by striking everyday objects inside a picnic basket, where embracing the uncertainty of machine learning opens up a playful and ambiguous music-making experience. During the design process, my encounters with birds grew into an attunement to them, leading me to include their calls in Picnic and to reflect critically on designing with non-human sounds. The third artefact, Table Manners, is an interactive music experience that invites participants to engage in music-making by striking absurdly large dinnerware and using their voices, exploring how a playful setting could take the pressure off participation.

Alongside three papers, I present the artefacts as a collection of designs in an annotated portfolio, reflecting on insights that emerged throughout the process. Together, the artefacts outline a design space for participatory music-making. Using the notion of meshwork, I describe this space as criss-crossed by a series of threads – namely, ambiguity, agency, playfulness, familiarity, size, persistence, bodies, voices, everyday objects, machine learning, and birds – that intertwine in knots that conceptually illuminate the artefacts. By examining these entanglements, the thesis reflects on designing for participatory music-making and offers insights into how designs can invite and support participation, while leaving room for what cannot be known in advance.
